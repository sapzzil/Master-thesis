# When Directional Accuracy Lies:  A Base-Rate-Honest Benchmark for LoRA-Adapted  TimesFM on Equity Forecasting

Taizhen Cheung  taizhen.cheung33@stu-mail.hunter.cuny.edu

###### Abstract

Large pretrained time-series models such as TimesFM are attractive starting points
for financial forecasting, but *raw directional accuracy is a misleading
scoreboard in equity markets*. An early LoRA adapter in this project appeared to
reach roughly <!-- MathML: <math alttext="80\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>80</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">80\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 80%80\% directional accuracy; we show this is not evidence of
forecasting skill. Over a long horizon in a rising market, a trivial “always-up”
rule attains comparably high accuracy *without using the input at all*. To
separate genuine skill from this base-rate artifact, we build a reproducible,
frozen-data benchmark with expanding *walk-forward* train/validation/test
folds, a stratified *held-out-ticker* split, honest baselines (zero-shot
TimesFM, always-up, random-walk, persistence, AR(1)), and paired significance tests
(McNemar, Diebold–Mariano) under Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control. We apply the *identical* method to two universes—a tech-heavy NASDAQ-100 and a broad
S&P 500—and report the headline metric as excess accuracy over the
always-up base rate. Three findings emerge and replicate. First, when the
historical <!-- MathML: <math alttext="\sim" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mo>∼</mo><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\sim</annotation></semantics></math> --> ∼\sim80% condition is recreated (a 2014+ bull window scored with raw
accuracy), the high number is a base rate of <!-- MathML: <math alttext="\sim" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mo>∼</mo><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\sim</annotation></semantics></math> --> ∼\sim0.70, and the fine-tuned model
scores *below* it. Second, on the honest benchmark, pooled LoRA showsno directional skill over the base rate at any horizon on either universe
(excess accuracy centered on zero, negative at the six-month horizon). Third, on the
pre-registered confirmatory test, per-sector specialization is significantly *worse* than a single pooled adapter (Diebold–Mariano <!-- MathML: <math alttext="p&lt;0.001" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo>&lt;</mo><mn>0.001</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p&lt;0.001</annotation></semantics></math> --> p<0.001p<0.001 on held-out
stocks at <!-- MathML: <math alttext="h=128" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>h</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>128</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">h=128</annotation></semantics></math> --> h=128h=128). Fine-tuning’s only measurable benefit is a statistically
significant reduction in point-forecast error relative to zero-shot TimesFM, which
nonetheless does not beat naive baselines and confers no tradeable directional edge.
The contribution is methodological: a defensible, fully seeded protocol that prevents
the base-rate trap, together with the replicated negative result it produces.

## 1 Introduction

Financial forecasting is an unusually treacherous setting for large time-series
models, because small apparent gains are easily confused with market drift, data
leakage, or evaluation artifacts. The problem is most acute for *directional* accuracy. A model that predicts “up” over a six-month equity horizon can look
highly accurate during a bull market even if it has learned nothing about the
relationship between past and future prices.

This project began with an apparent regression: a first TimesFM LoRA adapter
reported roughly <!-- MathML: <math alttext="80\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S1.p2.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>80</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">80\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 80%80\% directional accuracy, while later per-sector adapters dropped
toward <!-- MathML: <math alttext="50\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S1.p2.2.m2" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>50</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">50\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 50%50\%. Rather than treat the lower number as a failure, we treat the original <!-- MathML: <math alttext="80\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S1.p2.3.m3" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>80</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">80\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 80%80\% as the *suspect* result. The flaw is that the early evaluation compared
raw accuracy against an implicit <!-- MathML: <math alttext="50\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S1.p2.4.m4" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>50</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">50\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 50%50\% coin flip, even though long-horizon equity
direction is far from balanced: if most windows in the test period move up, an
always-up rule scores very well with zero skill.

The central question is therefore not “can the model reach <!-- MathML: <math alttext="80\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S1.p3.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>80</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">80\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 80%80\%?” but *“does the model beat the right baseline on the same windows?”* We design and
implement a benchmark to answer this defensibly, and we run it on two universes with
the method held fixed, so that the conclusion does not hinge on a single dataset.
Our contributions are:

1. 1.
      A frozen, checksum-versioned dataset and a fully seeded, reproducible training<!-- MathML: <math alttext="+" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S1.I1.i1.p1.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mo>+</mo><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">+</annotation></semantics></math> --> ++evaluation pipeline: every reported number traces to a committedraw.json and a run_meta.json recording seed, code commit, clean-tree
flag, and environment.

2. 2.
      A walk-forward train/validation/test protocol that never early-stops on the
test window, plus a stratified held-out-ticker split for symbol generalization.

3. 3.
      The headline metric <!-- MathML: <math alttext="\texttt{excess\_acc}=\text{model accuracy}-\texttt{always\_up}\text{ accuracy}" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mtext class="ltx_mathvariant_monospace">excess_acc</mtext><mo>=</mo><mrow><mtext>model accuracy</mtext><mo>−</mo><mrow><mtext class="ltx_mathvariant_monospace">always_up</mtext><mtext>&nbsp;accuracy</mtext></mrow></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\texttt{excess\_acc}=\text{model accuracy}-\texttt{always\_up}\text{ accuracy}</annotation></semantics></math> --> excess_acc=model accuracy−always_up accuracy\texttt{excess\_acc}=\text{model accuracy}-\texttt{always\_up}\text{ accuracy},
reported with block-bootstrap intervals and paired significance tests.

4. 4.
      A three-method, two-universe study (legacy pooled, walk-forward pooled, and
per-sector adapters; NASDAQ-100 and S&P 500) showing the negative result is robust
and resolving the pre-registered per-sector-versus-pooled hypothesis.

## 2 Background and Related Work

TimesFM [[1](#bib.bib1 "")] is a patched, decoder-only time-series foundation model
with internal reversible instance normalization (RevIN) [[3](#bib.bib3 "")] and a
quantile output head. We adapt google/timesfm-2.5-200m-pytorch with
LoRA [[2](#bib.bib2 "")], which inserts a small number of trainable low-rank matrices
into selected linear layers while freezing the pretrained weights, making it cheap to
train specialized adapters.

The closest motivation is the financial fine-tuning study of Fu, Hirano, and
Imajo [[4](#bib.bib4 "")], which reports a *modest* directional edge
(<!-- MathML: <math alttext="\approx 54\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S2.p2.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi /><mo>≈</mo><mrow><mn>54</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\approx 54\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> ≈54%\approx 54\% at short horizons) rather than extreme accuracy. That reference is
exactly why an <!-- MathML: <math alttext="80\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S2.p2.2.m2" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>80</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">80\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 80%80\% jump should not be accepted without checking base-rate
baselines, leakage, and out-of-sample generalization. We also draw on standard
forecast-evaluation practice: walk-forward validation, random-walk and persistence
baselines, MASE for point error [[5](#bib.bib5 "")], quantile coverage and
pinball loss [[6](#bib.bib6 "")], Diebold–Mariano tests [[7](#bib.bib7 ""), [8](#bib.bib8 "")],
McNemar’s paired test [[9](#bib.bib9 "")], the block bootstrap [[10](#bib.bib10 "")],
and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control [[11](#bib.bib11 "")].

#### Limitation (survivorship).

Both universes are *current-membership
snapshots*, not point-in-time constituents. This inflates the up base rate and the
long-horizon upward drift, so results are a benchmark over a frozen current-constituent
universe, not a live investable simulation (Section [8](#S8 "8 Limitations ‣ When Directional Accuracy Lies: A Base-Rate-Honest Benchmark for LoRA-Adapted TimesFM on Equity Forecasting")).

### 5.2 Held-out tickers

Within each sector, stocks are split <!-- MathML: <math alttext="\sim" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S5.SS2.p1.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mo>∼</mo><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\sim</annotation></semantics></math> --> ∼\sim80/20 into *seen* and *held-out* with a fixed seed (42), *fixed across folds*, so held-out names are never trained
in any fold. ETFs are excluded from the holdout pool and reported separately. Every
method is scored on both splits.

### 5.3 Normalization (leakage control)

A per-series log transform followed by <!-- MathML: <math alttext="z" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S5.SS3.p1.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mi>z</mi><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">z</annotation></semantics></math> --> zz-scoring is fit only on *pre-target
history*: the train period for validation windows, train<!-- MathML: <math alttext="+" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S5.SS3.p1.2.m2" intent=":literal"><semantics><mo>+</mo><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">+</annotation></semantics></math> --> ++validation for test
windows. No target-window observation is used. Direction is invariant to this monotone
transform; returns and point error use inverse-transformed prices.

### 5.4 Reproducibility

Training is fully seeded (seed <!-- MathML: <math alttext="=42" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S5.SS4.p1.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi /><mo>=</mo><mn>42</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">=42</annotation></semantics></math> --> =42=42 across Python/NumPy/torch/CUDA, with a seeded
DataLoader generator; the seed is set *before* LoRA initialization). The adapter
scored is the *best-validation* checkpoint, not the last epoch. Each run writes arun_meta.json manifest recording seed, git commit, a tracked-files-only
clean-tree flag, GPU, library versions, the full dependency freeze, hyperparameters,
and the per-adapter best epoch. All runs in this paper were produced at one code commit
on a clean tree, seed 42, on an NVIDIA A100 (torch 2.11.0<!-- MathML: <math alttext="+" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S5.SS4.p1.2.m2" intent=":literal"><semantics><mo>+</mo><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">+</annotation></semantics></math> --> ++cu128, Python 3.12); a
two-run reproducibility gate confirmed bit-identical Table-A accuracies. We do not
enable fully deterministic CUDA kernels (they raise on TimesFM operators), so results
are single-seed deterministic up to negligible GPU-atomic jitter; multi-seed robustness
is left to future work.

## 7 Discussion

The interpretive risk this project set out to avoid—mistaking raw directional
accuracy for skill—is exactly the trap the original <!-- MathML: <math alttext="80\%" class="ltx_Math" display="inline" id="S7.p1.1.m1" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>80</mn><mo>%</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">80\%</annotation></semantics></math> --> 80%80\% fell into. With an honest
base-rate baseline on identical windows, excess accuracy collapses to zero and is
negative at the six-month horizon: the equity uptrend, not the model, produced the high
accuracy, and this holds on a tech-heavy and a broad universe alike. The pre-registered
comparison adds a second, sharper negative: sector specialization is significantly worse
than pooling, so the intuitive “train a specialist per sector” recipe is
counterproductive here, most plausibly because each adapter sees far less data.

The one genuine, statistically supported effect is that LoRA reduces point-forecast
error relative to zero-shot TimesFM. This is a real but narrow benefit: it does not
beat naive baselines, does not produce directional accuracy over the base rate, and
does not yield a positive risk-adjusted return. Combined with the early best-validation
epoch, the picture is of a base model already near its useful operating point on these
series, with LoRA offering a small refinement of magnitude but not of direction. For
practice the lesson is methodological: report excess-over-base-rate, hold out tickers,
walk forward, and test paired significance before claiming a foundation model “works”
on equities.

## 9 Conclusion

Across two equity universes, with an identical seeded, reproducible method,
LoRA-adapted TimesFM shows no directional skill over the always-up base rate at any
horizon, zero-shot TimesFM is worse than the base rate, and per-sector specialization
is significantly worse than a single pooled adapter. Fine-tuning’s only measurable
