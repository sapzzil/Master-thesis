# ABSTRACT

### Prediction Intervals of Pretrained Time Series Foundation Models for Financial Returns:<br>Diagnosing Sources of Coverage Error and Training-Free Post-hoc Calibration

> Kim, Seungmin
> Dept. of Artificial Intelligence Convergence
> Graduate School of Applied Artificial Intelligence
> Hanyang University

Time series foundation models (TSFMs) produce prediction intervals without task-specific training, but whether these intervals keep their nominal coverage on financial returns has not been systematically tested. This thesis diagnoses whether miscoverage of TSFM 80% intervals stems from financial stylized facts or from the model and its usage settings, evaluates performance on real equities, and tests whether a training-free post-hoc calibration can restore coverage.

We generate 17 synthetic scenarios whose feature strengths (volatility clustering, heavy tails, regime-switching frequency and magnitude) are calibrated to ten U.S. and Korean large-cap stocks, varying one feature at a time under common random numbers. Four TSFMs (Chronos-T5, Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2.0) are evaluated on next-day log returns under four settings (21- or 252-day context; return or price input), and the same protocol is applied to the ten stocks over 2017–2026 (24,132 forecasts, 17,700 scored). Hypotheses and decision rules were specified before the experiments.

Even on feature-free data, TSFM coverage already ranged from 75% to 88% depending on model and setting, with half of the 16 settings outside the 80% ± 2 percentage-point band (five outside a ±3-point band). Raising feature strength to the strongest level (the strongest observed value, or twice the observed maximum for regime-switching frequency) moved coverage mostly within 3 percentage points. Only volatility clustering reduced coverage in some settings. Heavy tails and frequent regime switching increased it: at equal variance, a heavy-tailed distribution concentrates more mass near the center, so more observations fall inside an 80% interval; with the same distributional shape, the larger influence of tail extremes lowered the coverage of Chronos-2's 98% intervals. During the experiments we also found that the library's default sampling setting alone reduced Chronos-T5 coverage on return inputs to 14%; reported results use the corrected setting. Real-stock coverage differed from feature-free synthetic coverage by 1.1 percentage points on average (at most 3.6) across all 19 model-settings. On real stocks, quantile loss was larger than that of a historical-volatility Gaussian benchmark in 12 of 16 settings, and every one-year setting had larger loss than GARCH-t. Adaptive conformal quantile calibration (ACI-CQR), which leaves the model unchanged and adjusts the interval endpoints daily according to recent misses, restored average (unconditional) coverage to 80% in all 340 synthetic and 19 real model-settings, at the cost of intervals 5–48% wider than the oracle. In exploratory analyses, calibrated intervals brought realized volatility in a volatility-targeting strategy to 1.05–1.14 times the target and 10% VaR violation rates to 8.6–10.8%, although violation clustering remained in some settings.

Within the scope of these experiments, the coverage of TSFM intervals on financial returns varied more with model and input settings than with stylized facts, and feature-free synthetic coverage was close enough to real-stock coverage to flag the badly miscalibrated settings observed here before deployment, though not to predict small deviations. Distributional accuracy did not exceed the simple benchmark, reaching parity only in the best settings (one-year return input for all four models) and remaining below GARCH-t. If current TSFMs are deployed in practice, models and usage settings should therefore be checked first and training-free post-hoc calibration applied.

Keywords: time series foundation model, prediction interval, coverage, controlled synthetic experiment, conformal prediction, stock returns
